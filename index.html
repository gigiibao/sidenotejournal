side note

High school comes with a lot that no one really talks about. This website is intended to be a space where I write about the struggles, pressures, and experiences that come with being a high school student, while sharing different student perspectives and helpful resources. From academics and motivation to comparison, uncertainty, and everything in between, the goal is to bring attention to the parts of high school that often go unspoken and remind students that these experiences are more common than they may seem.

You're not alone.

Why Do Grades Affect My Confidence?

We're constantly told that grades don't define us. It's a comforting thought, right up until we receive one we don't like.

I know perfectly that a disappointing grade doesn't make me stupid. Yet somehow, when I see a score lower than I expected, my first thought isn't always that I struggled with the test. Maybe I'm just not as capable as I thought.

Perhaps grades affect us so much because they seem objective. Effort is difficult to quantify. So are improvement and potential. But a number at the top of a page seems definitive. It gives us something concrete to measure ourselves against, even when it doesn't tell the whole story.

And not to sugarcoat it, they do measure something. They can reflect how well we understood certain material or performed on an assessment. However, the problem begins when we allow them to measure everything else too, such as our potential, or even our worth.

Psychologists describe something similar through the concept of contingent self-worth. This happens when someone's sense of personal value depends on success or validation in a particular area, such as academics (Crocker & Wolfe, 2001).

When achievement becomes one of the main ways we reassure ourselves that we're capable, a good grade feels like confirmation. A bad one can feel like the opposite.

Ironically, a disappointing grade can hurt even more when we've genuinely worked hard for it.

If I barely studied, I can blame my lack of preparation. There's an explanation that protects my confidence. But when I've spent hours studying and still don't get the result I hoped for, that explanation disappears. It's harder to separate my performance from my ability when I know I genuinely tried.

There's also something unfair about how long disappointing grades seem to stay with us.

A good grade makes me happy, but the satisfaction doesn't always last very long. I accept it and eventually move on. Yet a disappointing grade can stay with me for much longer. I find myself replaying the mistakes I made and I can’t seem to get my mind off of it. Sometimes, one disappointing result seems to carry more weight than several good ones.

Psychologists call this negativity bias, where negative experiences or information can have a stronger impact on us than positive ones (Baumeister et al., 2001).

And perhaps that's why a single number can sometimes change how we feel about an entire subject, even when our previous results suggest otherwise.

What happens after the disappointment?

It's easy to tell someone not to take a grade personally. It's considerably harder to follow that advice when you're the one staring at the result.

In a 2008 study, Nussbaum and Dweck found that people who believed intelligence couldn't change were more likely to protect their confidence after failure by comparing themselves to people who did worse. Those who believed they could improve were more willing to look at their mistakes and learn from them.

But knowing that intelligence can develop doesn't automatically make a disappointing grade easier to accept.

We can't always control our immediate reaction to a result. We can, however, decide what happens afterward. Reviewing our mistakes, asking for feedback, or changing how we prepare might not erase the disappointment, but it can give us something more useful to do with it.

Research by Breines and Chen (2012) also found that self-compassion can encourage self-improvement. In one experiment, participants prompted to respond to failure with self-compassion spent more time studying for a subsequent test.

That finding challenges the assumption that being hard on ourselves is necessary for improvement. We can take our mistakes seriously without turning every disappointing result into another reason to criticize ourselves.

Grades matter. They can affect our academic opportunities, and it's reasonable to care about something we've invested time and effort into.

But there's a difference between recognizing that a grade matters and allowing it to determine what we believe about ourselves.

How Do I Stop Comparing Myself to Everyone Else?

You can be perfectly happy with your achievements until you find out what someone else has accomplished.

Maybe you were proud of your test grade until you heard what your friend got. Or you felt good about your extracurriculars until you saw how much someone else was doing. Suddenly, something you were satisfied with five minutes ago doesn't seem quite as impressive anymore.

And that's what I find so frustrating about comparison. Nothing about our own achievements has actually changed. We've just found someone else to measure them against.

Psychologist Leon Festinger explored this tendency in his Social Comparison Theory (1954). He suggested that people naturally compare themselves to others to evaluate their abilities and opinions, especially when there's no clear way to measure them (Festinger, 1954).

Psychologists also distinguish between upward social comparison, when we compare ourselves to people we think are doing better, and downward social comparison, when we compare ourselves to those we think are doing worse (Wills, 1981; Wood, 1989).

Comparison isn't always a bad thing. Seeing someone succeed can motivate us to work harder or try something new. But it can also leave us wondering whether we're doing enough, even when we were perfectly satisfied with our progress before.

And social media gives us many opportunities to wonder.

You've probably seen Instagram accounts that share students' university acceptances. My school has one too, and I genuinely enjoy seeing people get into universities they've worked hard for.

But there's something strange about scrolling through acceptance after acceptance, especially when they're from prestigious universities. You might not even be applying yet, but suddenly you're questioning whether you're doing enough to prepare for your own future.

There's nothing wrong with celebrating these achievements. But the posts only show us part of the story. We see where students were accepted, without necessarily seeing the rejections, uncertainty, or effort that came before.

It's easy to forget that we're looking at a collection of successful moments rather than a complete picture of everyone's experiences.

There's another psychological concept called pluralistic ignorance, which occurs when people privately feel or believe something but mistakenly assume that others feel differently (Prentice & Miller, 1993).

For example, we might feel uncertain about our academic abilities or future plans but assume that everyone else has everything figured out. Since people don't always openly discuss their doubts, it's easy to mistake their confidence on the outside for certainty on the inside.

Of course, some people genuinely are confident about their futures. Others might be struggling with things we know nothing about. Either way, we're making judgments about ourselves based on information we don't fully have.

How can we shift our focus back to our own progress?

I'm not convinced we can completely stop comparing ourselves. But we can question what those comparisons actually tell us.

When someone gets a higher grade or is accepted into a prestigious university, does that mean we're falling behind? Do we even have the same goals?

It also helps to look at how far we've come rather than only how far ahead someone else seems to be. Maybe we've improved in a subject we used to struggle with or finally tried something we'd been putting off. Those achievements still matter, even if nobody's posting about them.

And when it comes to social media, it's worth remembering how little we actually know from a single post. An acceptance announcement tells us where someone got in. It doesn't tell us everything they went through to get there.

The next time I find myself comparing my progress to someone else's, I want to ask whether I'm actually falling behind, or whether I've simply started measuring my achievements against someone else's goals.

Why Do Small Decisions Sometimes Feel So Overwhelming?

I've spent more time deciding how to spend my free time than actually enjoying it.

Sometimes, even the smallest decisions become a whole debate in my head. Should I join another extracurricular? Should I study for that test next week? Or should I just take a break? A normal afternoon becomes another thing I need to plan.

I think part of the problem is that at this age, we're constantly encouraged to make the most of our time, especially in high school. We're told to explore our interests, build our experiences, challenge ourselves, and prepare for our futures. And while there's nothing wrong with any of that, it can make even ordinary decisions feel like opportunities we can't afford to waste.

For example, choosing an extracurricular shouldn't be that stressful. But suddenly, I'm wondering whether it's something I'll genuinely enjoy and if it’s something that could potentially be listed in my applications, or whether I should be doing something more productive instead. Before I know it, I'm no longer thinking about what I actually want to do. I'm thinking about everything I might be missing out on by choosing it. This is what makes certain decisions so exhausting.

We want to make the best possible choice, even when there isn't necessarily one.

But how are we supposed to know which option is best when we haven't experienced either of them yet?

Of course, some decisions deserve careful thought. Choosing IB subjects, for example, can affect what we study later. But deciding how to spend a Saturday afternoon probably shouldn't require the same level of planning.

Something I've been trying to remind myself is that not every choice needs to be the best possible one. Sometimes, it's enough to choose something because I want to do it.

And if I end up regretting a small decision, that's fine too. I can make a different one next time.

After all, spending an entire afternoon worrying about how to make the most of it is probably the least productive way to spend it.

Can You Feel Lonely Even When You're Surrounded by Friends?

You can spend an entire day laughing with your friends and still come home feeling lonely.

It's a strange feeling. Nothing particularly bad happened. You had fun and you genuinely enjoyed yourself. But once you're home, there's still this feeling of being disconnected that you can't quite explain.

And maybe the most confusing part is that you don't really have anyone to blame. Your friends haven't done anything wrong, so why do you feel this way?

I think we often associate loneliness with being physically alone or having nobody to spend time with. But having company and feeling connected aren't necessarily the same thing. Sometimes, we can appreciate the friendships we have while still feeling uncertain about where we belong.

For example, you might get along with people from different friend groups but never feel entirely part of any of them. You enjoy spending time together, yet there's a lingering sense of being slightly out of place. And the difficult part is that nobody has to deliberately exclude you for that feeling to exist.

Other times, loneliness might have very little to do with the people around you. You might be perfectly happy with your friendships but still experience moments when you feel disconnected.

There might not be a clear reason for it.

I think that's what makes loneliness difficult to admit. When you already have good friends, saying that you feel lonely can almost sound ungrateful, as though you're suggesting the people around you aren't enough. So you might keep the feeling to yourself, or even question whether you have a reason to feel it at all.

But feeling lonely doesn't mean you're ungrateful for the people in your life. And it doesn't automatically mean your friendships are missing something.

What can we do about it?

Before deciding that something needs to change, it might help to understand where the feeling is coming from.

If you often feel out of place, maybe it's worth getting to know people you share more interests with or spending more time building the friendships you already have.

But if you're happy with your friends and still feel lonely sometimes, you don't necessarily need to change your social life. It might help to think about whether something else has been bothering you, or whether you've simply been having a difficult day.

And sometimes, there won't be an obvious explanation. That's fine too.

We tend to expect good friendships to make us feel connected all the time. But even the people who make us happiest can't guarantee that we'll never feel lonely.

Having people who care about you is something to appreciate. It just doesn't mean you'll always feel connected, every single day.

our story

where it all began

Side Note began with a school journalism article about the overlooked sophomore experience. Over time, it grew into a space for conversations about high school experiences.

the things that often go unspoken

it started with an article

It all started with an article I wrote for my school's journalism club, The Talon, titled ‘The Moment Sophomores Stop Feeling Invisible.’

The article explored how sophomore year is often overlooked in high school, right between the excitement of freshman year and the growing responsibilities of junior and senior year. Yet, when IB course selection begins, sophomores suddenly face the pressure of making decisions that seem to determine their academics and futures at just 15 or 16 years old.

Through my own experiences and observations, I explored the uncertainty, conflicting advice, and expectations that come with choosing between personal interests and future requirements.

Ultimately, the article highlighted how sophomores spend much of the year feeling invisible, only to suddenly face expectations about their futures when many are still discovering who they are and what they want to pursue.

from one article to a survey

After the article was published, I received responses from students, teachers, and even a school emotional counselor who shared how much the article resonated with them. Many expressed that it reflected experiences they had witnessed or personally gone through, making me realize that these feelings extended beyond my own perspective.

Their responses encouraged me to explore the topic further. In April of my sophomore year, I conducted a survey involving over 30% of my grade, asking students about their experiences with academic pressure, motivation, confidence, and the challenges of preparing for the years ahead.

what i realized

The results showed that many students shared similar struggles, even if they rarely spoke about them openly.

This made me realize that these experiences were not isolated. They were more common than I had initially imagined, yet often went unacknowledged. I wanted future sophomores to recognize that the uncertainty and pressure they might experience were not theirs to navigate alone.

from sophomore experience to side note

But the more I thought about it, the more I realized that these conversations shouldn't stop at sophomore year. The pressure to keep up, the uncertainty about the future, and the experiences we hesitate to talk about aren't limited to one grade.

That's how Side Note began to take shape: a space where I could continue exploring the things that often go unspoken throughout high school, through personal reflections, student perspectives, and helpful resources.

a note from me

I'm still figuring things out as I go, and I certainly don't have all the answers. But I hope Side Note can be a place where students recognize something familiar in what they read, feel comfortable sharing their own perspectives, and realize that some of the things they experience are more common than they think.

a note before you explore

Side Note is a personal space for sharing thoughts and experiences about growing up. The articles are meant to help you feel understood, not to provide professional advice or replace mental health support.

If you're struggling with your mental health, please consider reaching out to a trusted adult, school counselor, or qualified mental health professional.
